# Voice, tone and editorial

## Positioning
Nightlife Culture is a creative policy, research and design studio working across nightlife, music,
culture, place and the systems that shape them.

**Short proposition:** A creative policy studio for nightlife, music, culture and cities.

**Core belief:** Nightlife is culture. Nightlife spaces are not passive backdrops — they are talent
incubators, knowledge banks, gathering places, workplaces, testing grounds, civic assets and cultural
value generators.

## The voice
Nightlife Culture comes from inside the culture we're working to support. We've made the parties, stood on the dancefloors, built the communities, worked with the institutions and navigated the policies that shape what happens after dark.

Here's the thing. Nobody ever changed a funding decision with a paragraph that sounded like a planning consultation. So don't write one.

When you write for Nightlife Culture, you're writing for nightlife, to the people who decide about it. That's two jobs at once. Speak the system's language well enough that the spreadsheet can't ignore you. Never let it speak for you. The intelligence of nightlife doesn't need translating into institutional language to become legitimate. Our cultural knowledge is expertise, and we use evidence to strengthen it, not replace it.

Who's reading? A licensing officer who's been fighting our corner for ten years with no ammo. A funder who's never been out on a Thursday in their life. A promoter who'll clock in one sentence whether we actually get it. Write so all three keep reading.

- **Write from the floor, not the balcony.** You're not the tour guide or the anthropologist. Nobody needs the dancefloor explained to them.
- **Lead with what's working.** The story of the night is joy, and the evidence backs it up.
- **Say the big thing plainly, then prove it.** If you've got a number, make it massive. If you've got a hunch, call it a hunch and go and test it.
- **Be generous.** Arenas, trade bodies, councils: they're bringing people together too. We're not here to slag anyone off. We're here to show what everyone's missing.
- **Ask the question nobody else is asking.** Then ask the next one. Question the assumptions, expose the contradictions, and connect things that don't usually get considered together.
- **Have a laugh.** We take complex problems seriously without taking ourselves too seriously. Joy doesn't turn up in a lanyard.
- **Swear if it's appropriate.** Not because you think it makes you look edgy.

We're here to change the conversation about nightlife, and help the people who make it happen shape what happens next. If what you've written could have been written about any sector, by anyone, bin it and start again.

## Original thinking is not raw material to be sanitised
When you work from Carly's notes, recordings, transcripts or writing, preserve the argument before you touch the expression. Work from the originals, not from someone's summary of them.

- Don't swap a distinctive explanation for conventional sector terminology.
- Don't shrink an extended argument into a generic statement because the statement is shorter.
- Don't trade a challenging position for a diplomatic one unless the context genuinely calls for it.
- Keep the distinctions, qualifications, humour, analogies and examples.
- Say which words are Carly's exact words, which are edited quotations and which are newly drafted.
- Learn how she argues, then write new work that sounds like her. Lifting her lines is not the same thing.

The aim isn't to make Carly sound like a professional policy advisor. She already is one. It's to get her thinking across with the clarity, authority and personality she brings to a room.

## Think in systems. Speak in stories.
We move between the specific and the systemic, then come back to the specific to check the argument holds. A resident DJ playing twenty little gigs across a city. What happens when their regular venue closes? What do their line-ups tell us about the relationships holding a scene together? And why can't the way government measures cultural infrastructure see any of it? One familiar night out, turned into a serious question about networks. It's an editorial principle, not a slogan to repeat.

## How we build an argument
We don't open with abstract declarations about the importance of culture. We begin with something real. A room full of people. A promoter making something happen. A venue that doesn't fit the accepted definition. A statistic that tells half the story. A policy process that's somehow become more complicated than the problem it was meant to solve.

Then we dig into the assumptions underneath it. We connect cultural knowledge with economics, policy, design, research and lived experience, and we look for the contradictions. Who's been left out? What does the evidence actually say? Are we even asking the right question? Sometimes the most useful thing we can do is challenge the brief itself.

And then we explore what becomes possible when we look at it differently. The point isn't to explain the world as it is. It's to help people imagine and build something better.

**Observation → Assumption → Contradiction → Connection → Possibility → Action.** A diagnostic, not a formula. Start with the contradiction, or the possibility, or a story that only shows its hand at the end. What matters is that the reasoning survives the edit.

## The Carly test
Does this contain an actual idea, argument or observation that sounds like it comes from somebody who knows the culture and understands the system?

- Could any competent cultural consultancy have written this?
- Have we kept the argument, or replaced it with a general statement?
- Is there something specific, unexpected, insightful or challenging here?
- Have we lost the humour, energy or conviction of the original?
- Does the language respect the intelligence of both the cultural community and the institutional audience?
- Have we made the writing safer at the expense of making it meaningful?

If any consultancy could have written it, go back to the source. Not to add more personality: to recover the thinking that got removed.

Able to move between the dancefloor, the committee room, the funder meeting and the evidence base
without losing the truth of the culture.

**Emotional arc: joy → value → evidence → action.** This is the direction of travel, not a compulsory running order. Start wherever the argument is strongest: a story, a contradiction, a number. Just make sure the reader comes away knowing what people and places create, what the system is missing, what the evidence says and what can happen next.

## Rigour is not respectability
Intellectual rigour is not the same thing as institutional respectability. You don't need to write like an institution to show you understand how one works, and questioning the assumptions behind institutions is one of our strongest assets. Good writing still knows who's reading, gives enough context, owns its uncertainty and uses real examples. What it doesn't do is fake it: no borrowed slang, no chatty filler, no pretend intimacy.

## Voice characteristics
| Characteristic | What it means | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Culturally rooted | We understand nightlife from participation, not observation. We know the people, practices and relationships behind the statistics. | Explaining the culture to itself, or performing authenticity. |
| Intellectually curious | We follow unexpected connections, question assumptions and investigate what conventional analysis overlooks. | Answering the brief without asking whether it's the right brief. |
| Plainspoken and precise | We explain complex policy, economics and cultural theory without stripping out their meaning, and name evidence, uncertainty and structural problems accurately. | Consultancy sludge, unsupported certainty or softened truth. |
| Provocative with purpose | We challenge conventional thinking and name structural problems, and we can defend the argument. | Criticism for its own sake, or a challenge watered down into a diplomatic shrug. |
| Playful and irreverent | We have humour, character and a healthy disregard for unnecessary institutional pomposity. Humour exposes contradictions. | Jokes that belittle communities or people with less power. |
| Generous and collaborative | We recognise other people's knowledge, bring different perspectives together and create opportunities for collective action. | Defining people or places only through failure or need. |
| Joyful and ambitious | We see extraordinary possibilities in nightlife and believe they deserve serious consideration and investment. | Empty optimism, or abundance framing that hides structural problems. |
| Restlessly practical | Ideas are there to be tested. We prototype, learn, adapt and build. We want things to happen. | Ending on vague support or further engagement. |

## Three voice modes
Three registers of the same intellectual position, not three personalities.

| Mode | When to use it | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Carly, the founder | Thought leadership, talks, opinion pieces, personal reflections, project introductions | First person, distinctive, curious, funny, opinionated and grounded in lived experience. |
| Nightlife Culture, the studio | Website, proposals, collaborative projects, general communications | Collective, confident, culturally rooted and evidence-led. Still has personality. |
| Nightlife Culture, the evidence and policy authority | Research, technical documents, policy recommendations, investment cases and formal submissions | Methodologically rigorous, precise and transparent. The argument stays clear and culturally informed. |

Professional never automatically means removing personality. And keep it honest: don't imply a large permanent team, or that a whole sector has agreed a position, when neither is true.

## Tone by context
| Context | Tone | Emphasise |
|---|---|---|
| Public and sector comms | Direct, warm, culturally literate | People, place, lived reality, concrete meaning and action |
| Policy and technical reports | Precise, structured, evidence-led | Definitions, methods, assumptions, classifications, feasibility |
| Decision briefings | Concise, calm, decision-ready | Question, evidence, options, risks, recommendation, owner |
| Funding and investment | Ambitious but grounded | Need, value, method, outcomes, governance, delivery confidence |
| Research and academic | Transparent, rigorous, appropriately technical | Sources, methods, limitations, reproducibility, interpretation |
| Workshops and learning | Invitational, clear, paced | Purpose, steps, examples, participation, usable takeaways |

## Core language
Use where accurate, never as automatic slogans: collective joy · scenes · gathering places · cultural
infrastructure · informal knowledge · trust, care and graft · conditions to thrive · people who make
the night happen · shared asks · practical frameworks · sector knowledge · talent incubation ·
cultural value · evidence with soul.

These are ideas, not a phrasebook. The same goes for Carly's own images, like the spreadsheet guardians or the elephant burgers: they work because they come out of the argument. Sprinkle them in as decoration and the voice turns into a parody of itself.

## Technical language
Do **not** avoid specialist, academic or policy language where it is the most accurate language for
the job. Terms like regeneration, placemaking, resilience, creative industries, meanwhile use,
stakeholder and ecosystem have specific meanings in economic development, planning, research and
policy — use them where that meaning is relevant.

> Plain English is not anti-intellectual. It makes meaning available without removing complexity.

## Contested terms — check before using
| Term | Use when | Check for |
|---|---|---|
| Vibrancy | Defined through specific activity, time, place or indicator | Vague, exclusionary or visually coded |
| Resilience | Describes a system capacity, tested condition or defined risk | Responsibility shifted onto under-resourced people |
| Innovation | Genuinely new or newly applied | Whether "practical improvement" is more accurate |
| Inclusion | Group, barrier, mechanism and outcome are named | Whether it hides unequal power or access |
| Regeneration | Has a planning, funding or policy meaning here | Who benefits, what may be displaced, whose place is remade |
| Activation | Describes a specific intervention or programme | Whether it implies a place was empty before institutional action |
| Stakeholder | Useful in technical governance documentation | Whether people, partners, residents or workers is more accurate |
| Community | A defined group, place or relationship is meant | Whether differences in interest and power are hidden |
| Placemaking | Recognised planning term needed for the audience | Whether it erases people already making the place |
| Ecosystem | Interdependencies genuinely being analysed | Whether it replaces a more precise description |
| World-class | There is a stated comparison and credible evidence | Unsupported boosterism |
| Meanwhile use | A specific temporary planning arrangement | Whether cultural users absorb insecurity for others' benefit |
| Creative industries | The official sector definition is relevant | Which subsectors and classifications are in or out |

## Editorial standard
- Establish the argument early, but leave room for a story, a discovery or a reveal.
- Mix sentence lengths: long when you're exploring a relationship, short when you land the point.
- Break long sentences where ideas compete.
- Prefer concrete verbs to abstract nouns.
- Use headings and short sections to guide the reader.
- Use examples that are real and relevant.
- Cut filler, accidental repetition and AI-style platitudes. Keep deliberate repetition: it's rhythm and conviction.
- Do not invent specificity, statistics or quotes.
- Keep nuance where it changes meaning.

## Rhythm, emphasis and rhetoric
Nightlife Culture writing should have the energy and movement of a good conversation.

- Questions are welcome when they expose an assumption, introduce real uncertainty or bring the reader into the reasoning. Often they're the most important part of the argument, so don't cut them just to sound more declarative. Questions used as empty hooks go.
- Repetition can build emphasis and conviction. Remove the accidental kind, not the deliberate rhythm.
- Humour, understatement and irreverence are part of the voice. Use them naturally, not as decoration.
- Metaphors are there to clarify relationships and complex ideas, not to make ordinary writing sound creative.
- Bring a big argument back to a simple, familiar image when you can.

## House spellings
- Night time is two words, never hyphenated.
- Cultural value and cultural contribution are not interchangeable: cultural value is what something is worth to people and places; cultural contribution is what a particular person, venue or scene adds. Use the one you mean.
- British English throughout.

## Avoid AI voice
Never: "this matters" · "here is the bit everyone misses" · "in today's rapidly changing landscape" ·
"unlock the potential" · "at the heart of" · "more important than ever", unless genuinely earned by the content. Avoid the patterns
too: every argument turned into three short sentences, three abstract nouns or a predictable inspirational conclusion.

## What flattening looks like
What flattening looks like. The stronger versions aren't more aggressive. They're more specific, they have a point of view, and they're much harder to mistake for generic consultancy writing.

**Cultural value**
- Flattened: Small venues make an important contribution to local cultural ecosystems.
- Stronger: We tend to measure the importance of a venue by how many people it can fit through the door. But what if the most culturally significant room in a city only holds 75 people? What if that's where the next musical movement is being made? Perhaps the problem isn't the size of the venue. Perhaps we're measuring the wrong thing.

**Evidence**
- Flattened: Nightlife Culture brings together existing datasets to strengthen evidence-led advocacy.
- Stronger: We've got government datasets telling us one thing about a place and ticketing data telling us something completely different. One tells us how many businesses and licensed premises exist. The other starts showing us what people are actually programming. What happens when we put those two pictures together? And what might we find out about the people and places we've been overlooking?

**Collaboration**
- Flattened: We take a collaborative, human-centred approach to policy development.
- Stronger: I don't want to spend another three years consulting people about something they already know needs to change. I want to get the right people in a room, work out what's stopping us, agree what we're going to test and get on with it. And when we bring people back together, they should be able to see what changed because they were there.

## Evidence standard
Strong design must never make weak evidence look stronger than it is.

- Distinguish fact, estimate, interpretation, assumption and recommendation
- Cite the original source; state publication date, data period, geography and population
- Explain sector definitions and classifications, including SIC codes where relevant
- Identify proprietary, commercial and restricted data
- Describe primary research methods, recruitment, sample size and limitations
- Do not imply causation from correlation
- Avoid unsupported economic-impact multipliers and false precision
- Separate commissioned from independent evidence
- Record uncertainty, missing data, disagreement and methodological judgement
- Link recommendations to evidence and identify who can act

### Accurate evidence, strong argument
Two different jobs. Accuracy is about the evidence: what it is, where it came from, what it can and can't carry. Strength is about the argument we make with it. A sharp argument can stand on modest evidence, as long as we're straight about which is which. What we never do is let a confident argument make the evidence look stronger than it is, or let cautious evidence talk us out of making the argument at all.

### Recommendation test
Every recommendation answers: what is being recommended · why (evidence and reasoning) · who can act
(named institution, role or partnership) · what is required (authority, funding, capacity, data,
policy change) · when · how will we know (outcome, indicator, review) · what could go wrong (risk,
trade-off, dependency).

## Decision hierarchy
When instructions compete: **1** legal and ethical · **2** accessibility and user need ·
**3** factual and methodological integrity · **4** clarity and usability · **5** format conventions ·
**6** brand consistency · **7** stylistic preference.

Voice is not stylistic preference. The argument, the reasoning and culturally significant language sit with factual and methodological integrity, not at the bottom of this list. What comes last is decoration: a word choice that changes nothing.

> Accessibility does not dilute the brand. It decides where the brand should live.
