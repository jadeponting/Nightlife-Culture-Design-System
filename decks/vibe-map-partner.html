Nightlife Culture

Nightlife is civic infrastructure. Its purpose is collective joy.

We create the places people gather. We build scenes, connections and belonging. We give places their identity.

The basement venues that anchor a scene. The places you don’t wear your good shoes, but always meet a friend you didn’t yet know. The promoters taking a punt on something weird and wonderful. The people who turn up and make it happen.

The people make the place. We are the vibe creators.

Culture happens between people

A DJ responds to a room. The room responds to the DJ. An MC catches someone’s energy and takes the moment somewhere else. Something happens that none of them could have made alone.

The audience isn’t simply consuming a cultural product. It’s participating in its creation.

Someone takes an unexpected combination of sounds back to the studio. A promoter keeps gathering people until a night becomes a scene. Through participation, experimentation and collective imagination, culture keeps changing.

Our gathering places give us room to find ourselves and each other. A healthy music ecosystem makes space for different sounds, communities and ways of belonging.

We keep counting. What are we seeing?

We’re very good at counting the things that are easy to count. But are we measuring the things that matter?

Buildings. Businesses. Capacity. Jobs. Ticket sales. Useful measures, but they cannot tell us the whole story of a scene.

What’s the lifetime value of a resident DJ who keeps a community coming back? An arena isn’t everybody’s destination. Something small in the totals can be an anchor for the people around it.

You can have a city full of nightlife businesses without understanding its cultural life. When the activity sustaining a scene falls outside the categories used to recognise it, its needs are harder to make visible in policy and investment.

If we keep measuring the same things, we’ll keep telling the same story.

The change I want to see

I want the people who create the UK’s cultural life after dark to have greater influence over the decisions and investment shaping their places.

That is why I’ve founded Nightlife Culture: a creative policy studio for nightlife, music, cities and culture.

We bring cultural knowledge and evidence into the room with people who can act on it. Through research, advocacy, collaborative policy design and creative production, we build shared understanding and develop practical responses with the people affected.

My practice connects human-centred design with restorative economics: returning power to the people creating value.

Vibe Map is our first project. The larger ambition is to change how nightlife is understood, valued and invested in.

Carly Heath · Creative producer and cultural policy specialist

What if data could feel like a club flyer?

Vibe Map: data + design + storytelling.

A club flyer is packed with information. It tells you who’s playing, where to go, who’s behind it and whether this might be your kind of night. It’s an invitation.

What if evidence could tell the story of a place with that same sense of recognition?

Vibe Map is our first practical intervention: a shared evidence foundation and an interactive account of the people, activity, relationships and support systems that sustain nightlife.

We’re prototyping a free public-interest resource. The first view should tell you something meaningful about a place. Go deeper to explore the evidence and take it into a funding case or policy conversation.

The backing to build and test

The UK Dynamism Fund has backed Vibe Map to explore a different way of understanding nightlife through existing national evidence.

We will bring that evidence together, investigate cultural, social and economic approaches to value, and develop the prototype with cultural practitioners and decision users.

The public-facing artefact is one expression of the prototype. The research, methods we develop or reject, experiments and learning are equally important parts of the work.

I think of this like Lego blocks. Test something small, see what happens, refine it and build on what works. If a piece doesn’t fit, go back and work out why.

What happens when we ask different questions of the data?

Analysis

Can existing national data tell us a different story about how nightlife functions? Can events and ticketing information, viewed alongside public data about place, reveal patterns and relationships that either source alone misses?

Value

Can methods used elsewhere to understand cultural, social and economic value be adapted to the evidence we already have? How far can that take us before new quantitative research is needed?

Communication

Can we express the evidence in a way people understand and can use in decisions? Do nightlife communities recognise their scene in it?

Who needs to use it?

We are developing Vibe Map around three groups of decision users.

Politicians and policymakers

See the cultural life behind the businesses and premises they can count, and use that understanding in decisions about place, policy and support.

Night time ambassadors, trade bodies and nightlife advocates

Build common evidence and shared asks, while keeping the differences between scenes visible.

Investors and funders

Understand existing activity, cultural value and the support that could help it flourish.

The people who make nightlife help shape the questions, bring their knowledge and challenge the account. Their experience is essential to deciding whether the picture makes sense.

A picture can be technically tidy and still miss the point.

National evidence. A closer look at place.

The national evidence foundation brings together public data about place, population, economy and infrastructure with available events and ticketing information about what happens, where, when and who is involved.

Take a recurring club night. A premises record tells us about the space. Event listings may show a promoter returning, artists appearing together or activity moving between venues. Local knowledge helps us understand what those patterns mean, and what the records miss.

The evidence is national from the start. The close-up view helps us interrogate it.

Bristol and the West of England are our close-up testing context. We use that knowledge to challenge the analysis, then test whether the methods still hold when we look across the UK.

Five connected prototyping buckets

A. Decision-making
What do people need to decide, and does the emerging evidence help them?
B. Cultural truth
Does the picture ring true to the people who know the place? What is missing or distorted?
C. Methodology
How can we credibly understand economic contribution, cultural value and social impact using the evidence available?
D. Analysis
What patterns and relationships can we find by combining and interrogating datasets differently?
E. Technology
Can we turn those insights into something culturally resonant that people can explore, understand and use?

A challenge from the scene can change the analysis. A decision need can change what we show. The five buckets keep the work connected as we learn.

What changes when we can see it?

The data is the thing that needs to be done first. The interesting work is what becomes possible once we have it.

The future I’m working towards is one where a small venue can make its cultural significance understood, a funder can recognise activity that conventional measures overlook, and the people sustaining a scene can shape what happens to it.

A shared evidence foundation gives us something to bring into the room: a picture people can challenge, priorities they can develop together, and a stronger case to take to those who control resources.

Nightlife Culture will use that foundation to bring cultural communities and decision-makers together around policy, support and investment. Vibe Map tests how the evidence can help.

The ambition is to shift influence, investment and cultural power towards the people creating the value.

Why I’m building this

Carly Heath · Founder of Nightlife Culture

I’ve spent more than two decades making culture happen: promoting nights, producing festivals, working inside cultural institutions and developing public policy. I spent five years inside local government as Bristol’s Night Time Economy Advisor.

I know what it takes to get people into a room, build trust and turn an idea into something real. I also know what happens when the people making decisions cannot see the culture in front of them.

My practice brings creative production, human-centred design, evidence and restorative economics together. I’m interested in the connections between those worlds, and in returning influence and resources to the people creating value.

Nightlife Culture is where I’m bringing that experience together.

What’s the lifetime value of a resident DJ?

Someone plays the same room for years. People return, friendships form, new artists get heard and a scene develops its own sound. A career chart might see very little movement. A community might see an anchor.

We’re good at measuring the tallest trees. What about the ecosystem underneath?

Can recurring names, nights and connections in event data help us recognise that activity? Can the people who know the scene tell us whether our interpretation makes sense? What would make that a credible indication of cultural value?

Those are questions to test. Recurrence alone cannot tell us whether people feel welcome, what a night means to them or who the data leaves out.

Our creative policy design method

Human-centred design, restorative economics and participatory creative production run through the way we work.

Gather the people, hear their experience and agree what matters.

Map

Ideate

Develop possible answers with the people affected.

Prototype

Test

Find out whether it is recognisable, useful and helps people act.

Refine

Expand

Adapt what works in another context while preserving local identity.

The unexpected connection between people with different experience is often where a new possibility begins. We make room for that, and do the practical work needed to follow it through.
